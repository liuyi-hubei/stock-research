## First Principles

### Reality is multi-causal

Important outcomes rarely come from one variable. They arise from several forces acting together, often nonlinearly. A useful thinker therefore needs a limited set of powerful models from mathematics, accounting, economics, engineering, biology, psychology, and history, arranged as a lattice rather than isolated facts.

Mechanism: a model identifies one causal force; the lattice prevents that force from being mistaken for the whole system. Extreme outcomes often arise when several forces reinforce one another, which Munger calls a lollapalooza effect.

Boundary: collecting terminology is not multidisciplinary thinking. The models must explain causality, interact with one another, and be used repeatedly on real decisions.

### Avoiding stupidity has higher reliability than seeking brilliance

Prediction is difficult and opportunity is sparse. Removing paths to ruin, fraud, self-deception, and forced action is more controllable than forecasting exceptional outcomes.

Mechanism: irreversible losses truncate compounding, while avoided errors preserve optionality. This is why leverage, weak character, bad incentives, and problems outside the competence boundary receive disproportionate attention.

Boundary: error avoidance is not permanent inactivity. When a rare, understandable, asymmetric opportunity appears, excessive caution becomes its own error.

### Incentives are a causal variable, not a moral footnote

People, organizations, and professions adapt to what is rewarded, punished, measured, and socially approved. Stated intentions are weak evidence when incentives point elsewhere.

Mechanism: conditioning, self-serving bias, commitment, social proof, authority, and institutional survival reshape perception before they reshape behavior. Intelligent people can become especially effective at rationalizing incentive-compatible conclusions.

Boundary: incentives explain much but not everything. Character, professional norms, and system design can constrain them; a complete diagnosis still needs other models.

### Character is part of decision quality

Reliability, patience, honesty, and willingness to admit ignorance are operating assets. Envy, resentment, entitlement, and chronic self-pity corrupt both judgment and relationships.

Mechanism: good character reduces repeated agency losses and allows long-duration trust. It also makes learning possible because the thinker can revise a position without defending identity.

Boundary: admiration for character must not substitute for analysis of economics or competence.

## Decision Architecture

1. Define the real objective and time horizon.
2. Invert: list the outcomes that would constitute failure or ruin.
3. Determine whether the problem lies inside the competence boundary.
4. Identify the few models that govern the system and how they interact.
5. Map incentives for every important actor, including the analyst.
6. Run the psychology checklist: commitment, liking, envy, social proof, authority, availability, contrast, deprivation, overoptimism, and denial.
7. Establish base rates and use simple quantitative checks before narrative detail.
8. Search deliberately for disconfirming evidence, as Darwin did.
9. Examine accounting, leverage, liquidity, and tail paths that can force action.
10. Compare opportunity cost with the best available alternative.
11. Act decisively only when understanding, incentives, price, and survivability align.
12. Afterward, compare the original forecast with the result and perform a decision autopsy.

## Investment Model

### What to own

Prefer understandable businesses with durable advantages, honest and able management, favorable reinvestment economics, and a long runway. A great business can increase intrinsic value without requiring proportionate new capital.

### How to value

Value is the present economic worth of future owner cash flows, but exact calculation is often less important than a range that clearly separates attractive from unattractive. Opportunity cost is the practical benchmark.

### When to act

Wait for a small number of situations where the odds and consequences are strongly favorable. Concentration follows from rarity of insight, not from confidence as a personality trait.

### What destroys the thesis

- Hidden leverage or refinancing dependence.
- Management whose incentives reward volume, appearance, or empire building.
- A moat inferred from recent growth rather than customer economics.
- Accounting that obscures economic reality.
- A thesis requiring precise forecasts across many unstable variables.
- Investor dependence on social proof, borrowed conviction, or forced timing.

## Psychology as System Design

Munger's psychology is not merely a list of biases. Its practical use is to redesign environments:

- Align incentives before asking for better behavior.
- Use checklists and decision trees where omission is costly.
- Require postmortems that compare forecasts with outcomes.
- Use independent or blind review where confirmation bias is dangerous.
- Avoid auction formats and social settings that amplify commitment and rivalry.
- Communicate with reasons, because reason-respecting behavior is itself conditioned.
- Expect several biases to combine; do not assess them one at a time when the system is interactive.

## Evolution Across The Corpus

- DOC-035 frames wisdom as inversion and character: avoid the causes of a failed life.
- DOC-036 to DOC-039 build the cognitive machinery: model lattice, cross-disciplinary reasoning, forward and backward analysis, and competence boundaries.
- DOC-040 to DOC-045 move from individuals to institutions: fees, professional incentives, academic silos, and the failure of narrow expertise.
- DOC-046 supplies the behavioral engine explaining why intelligent people and institutions still fail.
- DOC-047 and later meeting notes integrate cognition with temperament, responsibility, and long-term conduct.
- DOC-054 and DOC-055 show the same framework applied to accounting credibility and public trust.
- DOC-056 converts the system into a repeatable checklist, while the meeting notes test it against actual companies and market cycles.

The corpus does not show a replacement of the early model. It shows progressive integration: inversion, models, incentives, psychology, character, and opportunity cost become one decision system.

## Tensions And Limits

- Concentration is rational only when competence is real; outsiders cannot infer competence from conviction or past returns.
- Avoiding macro forecasts does not mean macro forces are irrelevant. It means they rarely provide a dependable action edge.
- A checklist prevents omission but cannot manufacture understanding.
- Historical examples illustrate mechanisms but do not prove that the same quantitative relationship persists.
- Munger's strong judgments can tempt followers into authority bias, precisely the failure his framework warns against.

## Compact Operating Algorithm

Ask: What is the objective? What causes ruin? Which models govern this? Who is rewarded for what? Which psychological forces are active? What would prove me wrong? Can I survive being early or wrong? What is the opportunity cost? If the answer remains clear after those questions, act with patience and scale; otherwise move it to the too-hard pile.
