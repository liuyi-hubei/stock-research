# Sanhuyi: Deep Distillation

## Source Unit

Primary source: DOC-186, a third-party compilation of posts from roughly 2013-2023. The compiler explicitly notes possible omissions, errors, changing views, and the importance of original context. Treat the framework as a reconstructed practice, not a formally authored doctrine.

## First Principles

### Own a productive tree, not a quoted ticket

The tree metaphor separates two return sources. Fruit is business-produced cash and intrinsic-value growth; the rabbit is an unpredictable market revaluation. A sound investment must be worthwhile from fruit alone, while the rabbit is optional upside.

### Start from the worst plausible case

Cheapness is not protection if the company can fail, dilute owners, stop distributing cash, or require refinancing. Before estimating upside, ask what happens if the industry, management, and market environment all disappoint.

### Dividend payback is a discipline for connecting price to ownership economics

“Ten years of dividends to recover cost” is not a universal valuation formula. It is a conservative device for asking whether the business can transfer enough real cash to the owner, whether distributions can persist, and whether the entry cost is low relative to productive capacity.

### Leverage converts ownership into a timing bet

An unlevered owner can wait through quotation losses. A borrower must also be correct about the path and timing of price. Financing therefore introduces a different proposition from the original value thesis.

## Causal Model

Survivable balance sheet + durable demand + self-funded operations -> continued dividends or retained value -> ability to hold without market cooperation -> compounding through reinvestment.

Low purchase cost -> more shares acquired from each dividend -> faster growth of ownership -> less dependence on terminal selling price.

Leverage -> fixed payment and collateral constraints -> forced action during adverse quotations -> destruction of the waiting advantage.

## Decision Sequence

1. Describe the worst realistic industry and company outcome.
2. Test survival: debt, refinancing, fixed costs, dilution, and need for outside capital.
3. Normalize distributable earnings rather than extrapolate a peak year.
4. Estimate whether dividends and retained earnings can plausibly recover the purchase cost over a long period.
5. Identify exactly where return comes from: business cash, intrinsic growth, revaluation, or speculation.
6. Buy without leverage and at a cost that permits waiting.
7. Reinvest distributions when prospective return remains attractive.
8. Make few decisions; review business evidence rather than daily quotations.
9. Sell when survival, distribution capacity, opportunity cost, or valuation changes materially, not merely because price rose.

## Failure Modes

- Averaging down because the price fell while the original worst-case analysis was wrong.
- Confusing a high historical dividend with sustainable distributable cash.
- Treating a cyclical peak as normal earnings.
- Using “long term” to excuse management deterioration.
- Concentrating beyond one's financial or psychological ability to survive.
- Borrowing against an asset whose market path is uncontrollable.
- Measuring success only as buy price versus sell price and ignoring ownership accumulated through distributions.

## Evolution And Tension

The corpus contains both durable owner-thinking and situational macro or industry forecasts. The durable system is strongest where it concerns survivability, cash distribution, purchase cost, leverage, and patience. Macro forecasts and company-specific views should not be promoted to universal principles.

“Hold indefinitely” is contextual, not absolute. The compilation also recognizes selling when expected yield collapses, valuation becomes extreme, or a more productive ownership opportunity appears.

## Compact Operating Algorithm

If no market quotation were available for years, would the business survive and return enough cash to justify the cost? What is the worst case, and can both the company and the investor survive it without borrowing? If the answer depends mainly on a future buyer paying more, the investment is outside this framework.
