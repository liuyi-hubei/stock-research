## First Principles

### A stock is a fractional business ownership

The economic object is the business, not the quotation. Value depends on cash that can ultimately accrue to owners, including cash distributed now and cash retained at attractive returns.

Market price is an offer. It is useful when it creates a favorable transaction and irrelevant when it merely fluctuates.

### Intrinsic value is future owner economics, not accounting identity

Book value, GAAP earnings, EBITDA, and reported net income are inputs with changing relevance. Intrinsic value is the discounted economic cash that can be taken from a business over its remaining life without damaging its competitive position.

Owner earnings therefore adjust reported profit for non-economic charges, required maintenance investment, working capital, and the economics of retained earnings. Precision is false when the business itself is unstable.

### Margin of safety combines price, quality, and understanding

Early Buffett emphasized a large discount between price and conservatively estimated value, often with diversification and a catalyst. Later Buffett added the protection supplied by durable business economics, trustworthy management, and the ability to forecast the broad cash engine.

Margin of safety is weakened when a low multiple masks capital intensity, obsolescence, leverage, bad incentives, or an unknowable future.

### The best businesses convert little incremental capital into much greater value

Economic goodwill is valuable when a business can raise prices, retain customers, and grow earnings without proportional tangible investment. Such businesses can compound while distributing or redeploying cash.

By contrast, a business that must continually add capital merely to preserve unit economics may report growth while creating little owner value, especially under inflation.

### Capital allocation is the central managerial task

Every retained dollar competes with dividends, repurchases, debt reduction, internal reinvestment, acquisitions, and marketable securities. The correct choice depends on prospective per-share value, not corporate size or managerial excitement.

Repurchases create value only below conservative intrinsic value and with adequate liquidity. Acquisitions destroy value when managers pay for growth, prestige, or activity.

### Survival is a prerequisite for compounding

Berkshire maintains excess liquidity, avoids obligations that can force action, and treats insurance underwriting discipline as more important than premium growth. Low-cost or negative-cost float is valuable only if underwriting remains sound and claims can always be paid.

## Evolution Across The Corpus

### 1957-1969: discounted value, catalysts, and relative performance

The partnership letters separate investments into undervalued generals, workouts, and control situations. The process emphasizes conservative appraisal, a wide margin of safety, diversification where uncertainty remains, and performance measured across a market cycle rather than a single year.

### 1970s: inflation exposes the weakness of capital-hungry businesses

The analysis shifts from nominal accounting return to the capital required to maintain economic position. Retained earnings are valuable only when management can employ them at attractive incremental returns.

### 1980s: owner earnings, economic goodwill, and institutional behavior

The Berkshire letters formalize owner-oriented reporting, distinguish economic goodwill from accounting goodwill, and describe the institutional imperative: organizations imitate peers, resist contraction, and rationalize expansion. Management quality becomes inseparable from capital allocation and candor.

### Late 1980s-1990s: Mr. Market, competence, and the great-business transition

The corpus explicitly uses Mr. Market as servant rather than guide, sharpens the circle of competence, and acknowledges the limitations of cigar-butt investing. See's Candies and related examples show how pricing power, customer habit, and low incremental capital can dominate an apparently cheaper asset bargain.

This is not abandonment of value. It is a better estimate of what value consists of.

### 2000s: systemic risk, derivatives, and acquisition discipline

The framework expands to correlated financial risk, opaque derivatives, leverage, and incentives that privatize gains while socializing losses. Great businesses remain desirable, but valuation and structure must prevent permanent impairment.

### 2010s-2024: per-share value, trust, permanent capital, and succession

Book value becomes less representative as Berkshire owns more operating businesses. The letters emphasize operating earnings, look-through retained earnings, price-sensitive repurchases, decentralized operations, large liquidity reserves, and a shareholder base treated as long-term partners.

Trust is structural: truthful reporting, equal information, avoidance of quarterly target games, and successors able to preserve the culture are part of economic value.

## Business Analysis Sequence

1. Define the customer, product, payment, and competitive alternatives.
2. Normalize owner earnings across a cycle.
3. Estimate maintenance capital and working-capital needs.
4. Test incremental returns on retained capital.
5. Identify moat mechanism: brand, habit, cost, network, switching, regulation, or scale.
6. Ask whether the moat is widening and what could erode it.
7. Judge management through capital allocation, candor, incentives, and treatment of owners.
8. Examine leverage, liquidity, insurance or pension promises, and tail obligations.
9. Estimate an intrinsic-value range under conservative assumptions.
10. Compare price with value and with the best alternative.
11. Buy only with a margin that compensates for uncertainty.
12. Hold while per-share intrinsic value compounds and the thesis remains intact.

## Capital Allocation Decision Tree

- Reinvest internally when incremental returns are attractive and durable.
- Acquire businesses when quality, management, price, and integration structure are favorable.
- Repurchase shares when they trade materially below intrinsic value and liquidity remains ample.
- Distribute cash when no retained use is likely to create at least one dollar of value per dollar retained.
- Hold liquidity when alternatives are unattractive or obligations require resilience.

The objective is per-share intrinsic value, not revenue, assets, or corporate scale.

## Failure Modes

- Using reported earnings without maintenance-capital adjustment.
- Paying a high price because a business is excellent.
- Buying a statistical bargain whose economics are decaying.
- Treating diversification as protection from ignorance while holding many unexamined risks.
- Rewarding managers for size rather than per-share value.
- Repurchasing above intrinsic value.
- Allowing leverage or insurance promises to create forced action.
- Assuming recent growth proves a moat.
- Following macro or market forecasts instead of business economics.

## Tensions And Limits

- Precise intrinsic value is impossible for many businesses; those belong outside the competence boundary.
- A great business can still suffer technological, regulatory, or cultural decay.
- Concentration is justified by understanding and downside analysis, not by admiration for Buffett.
- Berkshire's permanent capital, reputation, tax position, and deal access are not fully replicable by an individual investor.
- Long holding periods are consequences of durable economics and tax efficiency, not a rule against selling when facts or opportunity cost change.

## Compact Operating Algorithm

What cash can owners take without weakening the business? How much capital must be put back? Why will customers keep paying, and what could change that? Will management allocate retained cash honestly and rationally? Can the company and investor survive adverse paths? Is the price materially below a conservative value range? If yes, buy as an owner and let compounding work; if not, wait.
